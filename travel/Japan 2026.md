## 🪄 Practical Tips
  
- **Rainy Season:** Late June is *tsuyu* (rainy season) — pack an umbrella and plan indoor activities (museums, shopping) on rainy days.

- **IC Card:** A rechargeable prepaid tap-to-pay transit card. Buy at the airport on arrival (¥2,000 — ¥500 refundable deposit, ¥1,500 usable credit). Reload at any station machine with cash.

- **Cards:** Suica, PASMO, or ICOCA — all work interchangeably nationwide. Get whichever is available at Haneda on arrival.

- **Use for:** subway, metro, bus, local JR trains, convenience stores (7-Eleven/Lawson/FamilyMart), vending machines, coin lockers.

- **Does NOT cover:** Shinkansen tickets or Hakone Freepass routes.

- **Phone instead of physical card:** iPhone 8+ supports Mobile Suica via Apple Wallet — tap to pay just like a physical card, works even with a dead battery. Load money via Apple Pay with a foreign credit card. Set it up before leaving home: open Wallet app → Add Card → search "Suica". Android only works if your phone has a FeliCa chip (US-market Android phones typically do not — use a physical card instead).

- **Apple Watch:** Series 3+ supports Suica in Wallet — double-click side button and tap your wrist at any gate, bus, or convenience store. Works independently from your iPhone (separate balance), even without your phone nearby. Setup: iPhone → Watch app → Wallet & Apple Pay → Add Card → Suica.

- **Kids Suica (for our 11-year-old):** Get the **physical Kids Suica (こども用Suica)**, not a Mobile/digital one. It auto-charges the **half-price child fare** on trains and subways — Mobile Suica only ever charges the adult fare, so a digital card overpays on every ride. Child fare applies through age 11 (elementary school); at 12 / junior high they switch to adult fare. Buy at a JR ticket office (midori-no-madoguchi) at Haneda or any major station — needs a passport/ID to verify the child's age (one-time). Reload with cash at any station machine, same as the adult cards.

- **Credit Cards & Apple Pay:**

- **Visa/Mastercard** — accepted at hotels, department stores, convenience stores, larger restaurants, and most tourist-facing shops.

- **Amex** — less widely accepted, don't rely on it.

- **Apple Pay** — works at convenience stores (7-Eleven, Lawson, FamilyMart), department stores, and most contactless terminals (Japan uses iD and QUICPay networks, both supported by Apple Pay).

- **Cash only:** small local restaurants, temples/shrines entrance fees, traditional shops, street food stalls, Hakone Freepass. Always keep ¥10,000–15,000 on you.

- **Note:** Apple Pay for Suica (transit) and Apple Pay for credit cards are separate functions on your iPhone — both work independently.

- **Cash:** Carry cash — many shops and ryokans are cash-only. Target ¥80,000–100,000 (~$500–625) total for 12 days (hotels pre-paid). Breakdown: ~¥15,000 (~$94) fixed costs (Freepass, day passes, entrance fees) + ~¥5,000 (~$31)/day for meals, snacks, and misc.

- **Best ATM card: Charles Schwab High Yield Investor Checking** — reimburses all ATM fees worldwide (including Japanese bank fees), no foreign transaction fees, live interbank exchange rate. No need to exchange money before leaving home.

- **Best ATMs in Japan:** 7-Eleven ATMs (accept Schwab Visa, English interface, open 24/7, found everywhere) and Japan Post ATMs.

- **Strategy:** land at Haneda → withdraw ¥30,000–40,000 at the 7-Eleven ATM in the airport → replenish at any 7-Eleven as needed. Keep ¥10,000–15,000 on you at all times.

- **Note:** Hakone Freepass (¥4,600) must be paid in cash at Odawara Station — IC cards not accepted for pass purchases.

- **Connectivity:** Rent a pocket Wi-Fi or buy an eSIM/data SIM.

## 🍔 Food to Try

  

### Tokyo

- **mipig cafe** — Japan's first micro pig café; friendly pigs you can hold and interact with. Entrance fee covers everything — no food/drinks required. Two convenient locations:

- **Harajuku Takeshita Street** — perfect fit for Day 4 (you're already going to Takeshita Street)

- **Ikebukuro** — alternative if Harajuku is full

- Pricing (per person): 25 min ¥3,300 (~$21) | 55 min ¥4,950 (~$31) | Kids 3 and under ¥1,100

- ✅ **BOOKED** — Harajuku Takeshita Street, Day 4 @ 11:30 AM (55 min). Reservation required, no walk-ins. Open 10 AM–8 PM.

- **Ramen** — rich noodle soup; try shoyu (soy), miso, or tonkotsu (pork) styles. Everywhere, from ¥800–1,500.

- **Sushi** — Tsukiji outer market or Toyosu for fresh breakfast sushi; conveyor belt (kaiten-zushi) is fun and cheap (¥100–200/plate).

- **Yakitori** — grilled chicken skewers, best in an izakaya (Japanese pub) in Shinjuku or Golden Gai.

- **Convenience store food** — 7-Eleven/Lawson onigiri (rice balls), egg salad sandwiches, and hot foods are genuinely excellent and cheap (~¥150–300).

  

### Hakone

- **Onsen tamago** — soft-boiled eggs cooked in the volcanic hot springs at Ōwakudani. A local specialty unique to Hakone, ~¥300 for 5 eggs.

- **Kaiseki** — traditional multi-course Japanese dinner; your ryokan-style hotel may offer this in the evening.

  

### Kyoto

- **Kaiseki** (懐石) — Kyoto's signature multi-course dinner, seasonally driven. Best experienced in Gion. Budget ¥8,000–20,000/person.

- **Tofu cuisine** (yudofu) — Kyoto-style simmered tofu; a local specialty especially around Arashiyama.

- **Matcha everything** — Kyoto is the home of matcha. Try matcha soft serve, matcha mochi, and matcha tea ceremony snacks.

- **Yatsuhashi** — iconic Kyoto sweet; thin cinnamon rice crackers or soft mochi filled with red bean paste. Great souvenir.

  

### Nara

- **Mochi** — pounded rice cakes; buy fresh from street stalls near Tōdai-ji. Watch out — the deer will try to steal it.

- **Kakinoha-zushi** — local Nara specialty: sushi wrapped in persimmon leaves.

  

### Osaka

- **Takoyaki** (たこ焼き) — octopus-filled batter balls, topped with sauce and bonito flakes. Osaka's street food icon, ~¥500–600 for 8 pieces. Best in Dōtonbori.

- **Okonomiyaki** (お好み焼き) — savory pancake with cabbage, meat, and toppings. Osaka-style is mixed together (vs. Hiroshima-style layered). ~¥800–1,200.

- **Kushikatsu** — deep-fried skewers of meat and vegetables, dipped in sauce. Rule: no double-dipping. A Osaka staple, best in Shinsekai district.

- **Ramen (Osaka-style)** — lighter, soy-based broth; different character from Tokyo ramen.

  

### 🏪 7-Eleven (Konbini) — Best Things to Grab

*Genuinely excellent and cheap — great for breakfasts, train rides, late nights, and the kid. Pay with Apple Pay or Suica. Lawson & FamilyMart are just as good; chain favorites noted where they differ.*

- **Onigiri (rice balls), ~¥150–200** — the staple. Top fillings: tuna-mayo (sea-chicken), grilled salmon, ume (pickled plum), kombu. Look for the easy-open seaweed wrapper.

- **Tamago sando (egg salad sandwich), ~¥250** — famously fluffy, creamy; a must-try (Anthony Bourdain–approved). The fruit sando (whipped cream + fruit) is also a treat.

- **Fried chicken at the counter** — 7-Eleven's *Nanachiki*; FamilyMart's **Famichiki** is the cult favorite. Hot, crispy, ~¥200.

- **Nikuman / steamed buns** (hot case by the register) — pork bun, pizza bun, curry bun. ~¥150.

- **Bento boxes & pasta, ~¥500–700** — surprisingly good rice bowls, katsu, yakisoba; staff will microwave them for you.

- **Egg & dairy:** soft-boiled seasoned eggs (ajitama), pudding (purin), and drinkable yogurts.

- **7-Eleven sweets** — the **mochi/warabi-mochi and matcha desserts** are standouts; their premium ice cream and **mochi-wrapped ice cream** are excellent.

- **Drinks** — cold barley tea (mugicha), bottled matcha/green tea, canned coffee (BOSS), and the chilled coffee cups you fill at the machine.

- **For the kid:** onigiri, Famichiki, melon pan, pudding, and the ice cream case are all easy wins.

## 🛍️ Shopping — Stores to Stop By

*Found all over your route (Shibuya, Akihabara, Shinsaibashi/Dōtonbori, station malls). Donki is the big planned stop; the others are browse-if-you-pass-one.*

  

- **Don Quijote (ドンキ / "Donki")** — chaotic 24h discount megastore. **Tax-free for tourists** (passport + spend over ¥5,000 → skip the 10% tax). Best for souvenirs (novelty Kit Kats, Japanese snacks, character goods), cosmetics/skincare, cheap sock multi-packs, and novelty/character socks. Flagship **Dōtonbori store (yellow Ferris wheel) ~10-min walk from the Namba hotel** — main souvenir haul on Days 10–11. Also big stores in Shibuya & Akihabara (Tokyo).

- **3COINS (スリーコインズ)** — tidy ¥330 shop, design-forward cute lifestyle goods. **Best place for socks** (cute designs, seasonal patterns, good cotton) plus small packable gifts. Browse if you spot one in Shibuya/Shinsaibashi or a station mall.

- **Uniqlo** — high-quality basics; go-to for durable **plain socks** and travel essentials. Everywhere.

- **MUJI** — minimalist quality basics; also great for **plain socks**, stationery, and simple gifts. Everywhere.

  

**Socks quick guide:** cute/seasonal → **3COINS** · cheap bulk or novelty → **Donki** · durable plain basics → **Uniqlo / MUJI**.

### 🗓️ Day 9 — Mon, June 29 (Nara Day Trip)

*Everything sits inside/around Nara Park — walk it as one loop, west→east→south. ~15 min walk from station to Kōfuku-ji.*

- **7:30 AM:** Breakfast buffet at hotel.

- **8:30 AM:** JR Nara Line Rapid from Kyoto → Nara (~50 min, IC card). Beat the day-tripper crowds.

- **9:30 AM — Kōfuku-ji:** ancient temple with a five-story pagoda (UNESCO) — closest to the station, first stop.

- **11:00 AM — Tōdai-ji Temple:** 8th-century temple housing the 15-meter bronze Great Buddha (Daibutsu, 752 CE) — the main event, in the north of the park.

- **1:00 PM — Kasuga Taisha:** Shinto shrine with hundreds of hanging bronze and stone lanterns — east end of the park, through wooded paths.

- **2:00 PM — Naramachi:** old merchant quarter south of the park; traditional machiya houses, cafés, kakinoha-zushi (local sushi in persimmon leaves) for a late lunch.

- **~5:00 PM:** Return to Kyoto.
